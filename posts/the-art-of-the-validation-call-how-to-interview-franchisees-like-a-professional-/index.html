The Art of the Validation Call: How to Interview Franchisees Like a Professional Investigator

2026-05-23 · Reviews · Tags: The, franchiseopportunity, reviews

The Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) is a treasure trove of data. It gives you the financial performance representations, the litigation history, and the contact information for every current and former franchisee. But here is the secret that separates successful buyers from those who end up with regrets: the FDD is a starting point, not the finish line. The real due diligence happens when you close the PDF and pick up the phone. The validation call is your most powerful tool, yet most prospective buyers treat it like a casual chat with a stranger. They ask surface-level questions about revenue and 'how do you like it,' and they hang up with a fuzzy feeling instead of hard data. This article is your field manual for transforming those conversations into a rigorous investigation that uncovers the truth about a franchise system—before you invest a dime.

Preparing for the Interrogation: Research Before You Dial

Walking into a validation call without preparation is like a detective interviewing a suspect without reading the case file. You will miss the inconsistencies, and you will fail to ask the follow-up questions that reveal the real story. Before you dial a single number, you need to build a profile of the franchise system and the specific owner you are about to contact.

Start with the FDD's Item 7 (your estimated initial investment) and Item 19 (financial performance representations). If the franchisor provides a range of revenue or profit figures, write them down. Your goal is to compare the owner's actual experience against these official numbers. Next, look at Item 20, which lists the current and former franchisees. Pay special attention to the turnover rate. A high number of closures or transfers in a specific region might indicate a territory problem. If you notice a cluster of former owners from the same state, that is a red flag worth investigating.

Before the call, prepare a list of specific, open-ended questions tailored to the owner's situation. Are they a single-unit owner? Ask about the realities of being an owner-operator. Are they a multi-unit operator? Ask about the challenges of scaling and hiring managers. Your research should also extend to the local market. If you are looking at a home services franchise, check the local housing data. If it is a food franchise, look at the local commercial real estate trends. This context will help you interpret the owner's answers and spot potential red flags they might not mention directly.

The First Call: Separating the Hype from the Hard Numbers

The first call is about establishing a baseline of truth. You are not there to make a friend; you are there to gather evidence. Begin by introducing yourself and confirming the owner's name, location, and how long they have been in the system. This immediately sets a professional tone and gives you a data point to verify against the FDD.

Your first question should always be about the gap between expectations and reality. Ask, "What did the franchisor tell you to expect in your first year, and what actually happened?" This is a powerful question because it reveals the quality of the franchisor's training and the accuracy of their promises. Listen for specific numbers. A vague answer like "it was a bit slower than I thought" is not useful. Push for specifics: "By what percentage were your first-year sales below the Item 19 average?"

Next, drill into the unit economics. You need to understand the real profit margin, not just the gross revenue. Ask about the cost of goods sold, labor as a percentage of sales, and the actual rent they are paying. If the owner is reluctant to share exact figures, ask for a percentage range. For example, "What percentage of your monthly revenue goes to payroll?" This information is gold because it allows you to build a realistic pro forma for your own location. You should also ask about the 'hidden' costs that never appear in the FDD: local advertising cooperative fees, unexpected maintenance, or the cost of complying with new corporate mandates.

Probing the Intangibles: Culture, Support, and the 'Franchisor Relationship'

Once you have a handle on the financials, shift your focus to the relationship between the franchisor and the franchisee. This is where the true character of a franchise system is revealed. The FDD cannot tell you if the corporate office is responsive or if they treat owners like partners or like numbers.

Ask about a specific time they needed help. "Can you tell me about a time you had a major operational crisis—like a flood, a PR issue, or a supply chain disruption? How did the franchisor respond?" The answer to this question is incredibly revealing. A good franchisor will have a clear protocol and will send help immediately. A weak one will leave you to fend for yourself. You should also ask about the annual conference and ongoing training. Is it a genuine learning experience or just a sales pitch for new products? Are the field consultants knowledgeable, or are they just checking boxes?

Another critical area is the culture of the system. Ask about the communication style. Is the franchisee advisory council actually listened to, or is it a rubber stamp for corporate decisions? You can also ask about the 'vibe' of the owner community. Is it collaborative or competitive? Do owners share best practices, or do they guard their secrets? The answers to these questions will tell you if you are joining a supportive network or a collection of isolated entrepreneurs who just share a brand name.

The Art of the Follow-Up: Uncovering the 'Real' Story

The first call will give you a surface-level understanding. The follow-up is where you dig for the buried treasure. After you have spoken to three or four owners, patterns will start to emerge. This is when you need to go back to the most insightful owners and ask for more detail. Do not be afraid to call them a second time. Most owners are happy to talk, especially if they are proud of their business.

This second round of calls is for verification. If one owner mentioned a problem with a specific supplier, call another owner in a different state and ask if they have the same issue. If one owner raved about a particular marketing campaign, ask another if they saw the same results. Your goal is to separate one-off complaints from systemic issues. A single bad experience is a data point; a pattern is a warning.

You should also ask for specific documentation. Ask the owner if they are willing to share their profit and loss statement (P&L) for the last year. Many will say no, but some will say yes, especially if you have built a good rapport. If you can get even a redacted P&L, you will have a massive advantage over other buyers. You can also ask for a copy of their operations manual or their local marketing plan. This level of detail will give you a clear picture of the day-to-day reality that no sales brochure can provide.

Reading the Silent Signals: What They Don't Say

During your calls, you must be an active listener, paying attention not just to the words but to the tone, the pauses, and the energy. The most important information often lies in what is left unsaid. If an owner is enthusiastic about the business but hesitates when you ask about the franchisor's support, that hesitation is a red flag. If they say "It's a great system" but their voice is flat and monotone, they are probably not being entirely honest with themselves or with you.

Listen for the 'we' versus 'they' language. Owners who feel like partners will say "we are rolling out a new menu" or "we are trying a new marketing tactic." Owners who feel like victims will say "they are making us do this" or "they never listen." The language is a direct window into the relationship. Also, pay attention to the owner's energy level. Are they exhausted and burned out, or do they seem energized by the challenges? This is a critical indicator of the lifestyle the franchise demands.

Finally, always ask the 'exit' question. "If you knew then what you know now, would you buy this franchise again?" This is the ultimate test. A yes, without hesitation, is a strong positive signal. A 'maybe' or a 'it's complicated' is a huge warning. You should also ask, "What is your plan for the next five years?" If they are planning to sell or get out, you need to understand why. Their exit strategy is your entry strategy, and you need to know if the system has a healthy resale market or if owners are stuck.

Synthesizing the Evidence: Building Your Go/No-Go Decision Matrix

After conducting 8-10 validation calls, you will have a mountain of qualitative and quantitative data. The final step is to synthesize this information into a clear decision. Do not rely on your gut feeling; create a simple scoring matrix. List the key criteria for your success—such as profitability, franchisor support, work-life balance, and growth potential—and score each owner's feedback on a scale of 1 to 5. This will help you see the overall pattern objectively.

Compare the feedback against the claims in the FDD and the marketing materials. If the reality consistently falls short of the promise, that is a deal-breaker. If the reality matches or exceeds the promise, you have found a strong candidate. Also, look for the 'one bad apple' versus a systemic issue. If 8 out of 10 owners are happy and profitable, but 2 are struggling, find out why. Are those 2 in bad territories, or did they not follow the system? This distinction is crucial.

Remember that the goal is not to find a perfect franchise—they do not exist. The goal is to find a franchise where the risks are known, understood, and acceptable to you. The validation call is your chance to turn the unknown into the known. By approaching it with the rigor of a professional investigator, you are not just buying a business; you are making an informed decision that will shape your financial future and your daily life.

Conclusion

The validation call is the single most important step in your franchise due diligence process. It is the bridge between the polished promises of the FDD and the gritty reality of running the business. By preparing meticulously, asking probing questions, and listening for the silent signals, you can uncover the true DNA of a franchise system. You will learn not just if a business is profitable, but if it is profitable for *you*. You will discover if the culture is a fit, if the support is real, and if the lifestyle aligns with your goals. This process will not eliminate all risk, but it will eliminate the risk of the unknown. So, pick up the phone, do your detective work, and invest with the confidence that comes from knowing you have left no stone unturned.
